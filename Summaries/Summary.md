This thesis is built around a single organising idea: information processing in the brain is not implemented by fixed, stimulus-triggered computations running on an otherwise silent substrate, but by the continuous, dynamic reorganisation of ongoing neural activity. This principle motivates the thesis's central question: how does the dynamical reorganisation of cortical activity interact with, and shape, the information-processing properties of cortical networks? This question is addressed by combining experimental work, primarily in the awake, behaving mouse sensory cortex, with theoretical and computational modelling.

The **past research** is organised into four parts. The first examines how cellular and synaptic mechanisms shape single-neuron integration under the fluctuation-driven, "in vivo-like" activity regime that characterises the awake cortex. The second part identifies and models the diverse network activity regimes present during wakefulness, moving beyond the classical  balanced-network picture to characterise sparse, afferent-dominated and recurrent-dominated regimes, and developing a method to classify these fine-grained cortical states directly from local field potential signals. The third part dissects the interneuronal circuits underlying these state-dependent dynamics. The fourth part integrates these cellular and circuit-level findings into mesoscopic mean-field models, validated against voltage-sensitive dye imaging and used to explain the neural basis of the apparent-motion illusion, and subsequently generalised beyond the cortex.

Building on this foundation, the **research projects** section outlines four future directions. The first tests the hypothesis that different behavioural states support different, complementary modes of visual encoding in mouse V1, using a combinatorial stimulus set designed to dissociate several axes of sensory statistics. The second develops a theoretical, thalamo-cortical spiking-network framework linking dynamical regimes to computational properties, aiming to identify the minimal circuit ingredients required for flexible computation. The third investigates the cellular and developmental mechanisms underlying inhibitory specialisation, including the differential role of NMDA receptors in interneurons and how their distinct dendritic integration programmes emerge over development, with direct relevance to schizophrenia-associated interneuron dysfunction. The fourth focuses on layer 1 interneurons, examining their largely unresolved role in shaping sensory representations in visual cortex, beyond their established involvement in associative learning and state-dependent gating.

Across all four parts, the thesis pursues a consistent strategy: linking microscopic, biophysical detail to macroscopic, functionally relevant dynamics, using theory and experiment jointly rather than in isolation. 
Taken together, this body of work argues that the flexibility of cortical computation is not a peripheral feature of neural activity, but is implemented by specific, identifiable cellular and circuit mechanisms, chiefly the cell-type-specific engagement of distinct inhibitory interneuron populations, and that understanding this flexibility requires theoretical frameworks capable of spanning single synapses to large-scale network dynamics.
